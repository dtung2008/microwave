1.1 Why a 1939 paper calculator still runs the industry

Three claims for today

  1. The entire passive universe fits in a disk.
    Γ = (z−1)/(z+1) maps every impedance with R ≥ 0 into |Γ| ≤ 1. Nothing legal leaves.
  2. Circuit moves are geometric moves.
    A line rotates you; series elements ride one circle family, shunt elements another. Matching = two moves that end at the center.
  3. A perfect match is a resonance you built — billed in bandwidth.
    Both designs today are exact at 2.4 GHz (|Γ| ≈ 10⁻¹⁶). The drama is 400 MHz away.

Every VNA (vector network analyzer) still boots with Smith's picture on screen — not nostalgia: it is the coordinate system microwave design is legible in.

Passive means R ≥ 0 means |Γ| ≤ 1. Impedance is unbounded; reflection is not — Γ is the honest currency, which is why a VNA measures Γ and computes Z, never the reverse. Hour 3: 4000 random passive loads, max |Γ| = 0.999963.

1.2 The bilinear map, first-principles pass

Why circles map to circles

  • Γ(z) is a Möbius map: translations + scalings + one inversion w → 1/w.
  • Write any circle as α(u²+v²) + βu + γv + δ = 0. Inversion swaps α ↔ δ — same form. Lines are circles with α = 0 (through ∞).
  • So the impedance grid lands as two circle families:

const r: center (r/(1+r), 0), radius 1/(1+r)
const x: center (1, 1/x), radius 1/|x|
all tangent at Γ = +1 — the image of infinity, where the far half-plane crowds together

Stare for sixty seconds and the chart stops being a mandala and becomes graph paper.

1.3 Reading the chart

The chart — with our antenna on it

Plot ZL = 36 − j21 Ω (homework 2's patient) in four moves:

  1. normalize: zL = 0.72 − j0.42
  2. find the r = 0.72 circle
  3. find the x = −0.42 arc (lower half = capacitive)
  4. the intersection is the point — no algebra

The SWR circle (SWR = standing wave ratio): center through zL. Everything a lossless line can do to this load lives on it. The circle is the budget; matching is the escape.

The cable had rotated the point around the SWR circle: right circle, wrong angle. Reference planes are formalized in L4 (de-embedding); today's version: know which plane your point lives at before you move a millimeter.

1.4 Motion

A length of line rotates you — clockwise, 2βℓ, λ/2 per lap

Γin(ℓ) = ΓL e−j2βℓ (β = 2π/λ; ℓ toward the generator)
  • ℓ = λ/8 (90° on the chart): Zin = 28.4 + j6.0 Ω
  • ℓ = λ/4 (180°): point inversion → Zin = 51.8 + j30.2 = Z₀²/ZL — lecture 2's inverter, proved in one sentence
  • real-axis crossings: 27.8 Ω = Z₀/SWR at 0.097λ (v-min) · 89.9 Ω = Z₀·SWR at 0.347λ (v-max)

Why clockwise: the reflected wave has farther to travel back — e−j2βℓ, phase decreases toward the generator. The 2× and the direction are the two things everyone gets wrong once.

1.4 The admittance chart

y lives diametrically opposite z — and the three-move instruction set

  • Shunt elements add admittance: y = 1/z. Inverting = rotating 180° → y's point is z's point reflected through the center (the λ/4 fact, frequency-free).
  • Our antenna: yL = 1.036 + j0.605.

Sign discipline: a capacitive load has negative reactance but positive susceptance — +j means capacitive in admittance country. Hour 3's deliberate bug violates exactly this currency rule.

The complete instruction set:

line → rotate on the SWR circle (free)
series L/C → slide along a constant-r circle
shunt L/C → slide along a constant-g circle

Matching is a two-instruction program written in these moves. Hour 2 writes it twice.

1.4 The obligatory objection

"Isn't the chart obsolete? Software does this."

Backwards. Software plots on the chart. The chart is the coordinate system, not the calculator.

  • Every VNA screen, every antenna datasheet, every matching discussion at a whiteboard — happens on this disk.
  • Lecture 11: amplifier stability circles, noise circles, gain circles — all drawn here; you will pick a design point by reading them.
  • The engineer who can't read the chart is the one the software is obsoleting.

Smith drew it in 1939 because he was tired of hand-computing complex arctangents [R19]. The reason it survived: reflection, unlike impedance, is bounded — the disk is the honest currency.

1.5 Recap

Hour 1 in three sentences

  • The chart is the bilinear image of every passive impedance — circles to circles, rim is reactance, center is home.
  • Lines rotate you clockwise toward the generator at 2βℓ; λ/4 is inversion — which is also why admittance lives diametrically opposite.
  • Series rides r-circles, shunt rides g-circles: three moves, the whole instruction set.

Break. Hour 2 uses exactly these moves to put the antenna in the center of the disk — twice.

Hour 2

Matching, Two Ways

Why match · the two-move principle · the L-section from chart geometry · single-stub tuning · the price, paid in bandwidth

2.1 Why match

Honestly: 0.37 dB — and everything that matters more

Unmatched, the antenna already delivers 91.9% (−0.37 dB mismatch loss). The real purchases:

  • SWR 1.80 → 1.00: the feed line stops carrying standing waves (homework 2 showed what those cost over 10 m).
  • The transmitter sees 50 Ω resistive, not a load that wanders with every cm of cable (L11: amplifier gain, noise, stability all depend on what they see).
  • Downstream parts get the impedance they were characterized at.

Matching is less about power than about making the system composable.

One honest distinction: today we match the load to the line (drive Γ = 0). Conjugate-matching a source for maximum power (Zs*) is a different problem — they coincide while sources are 50 Ω resistive, the standing assumption until L11 reopens it for amplifiers.

2.1 The two-move principle

Last move picks the rail; first move gets you to the rail

  • Target: the center, y = 1 + j0.
  • A final shunt element only changes b → it must start on the g = 1 circle.
  • A final series element only changes x → its rail is the r = 1 circle.
  • Every topology in this course — today's L-section and stub, L6's multisections — is a path built backwards from the center along legal rails.
2.2 The L-section

Two components, one order question — the chart answers it

Region rule: the topology whose first move can reach its rail. RL < Z₀ → series-first (reach g = 1). RL > Z₀ → shunt-first (reach r = 1). The other one's square root goes negative — algebra saying "you can't get there on that rail."

The homework designer must discover the region — the checker feeds it 120 + j90 Ω to make sure.

2.2 The L-section

One number predicts the whole band: Q

Q = √(Z₀/RL − 1) = √(50/36 − 1) = 0.62
  • A lazy, forgiving resonance — because 36 Ω is not far from 50.
  • Matching 5 Ω would cost Q = 3 and the band would show it.
  • Hour 3 measures what Q = 0.62 buys: worst in-band RL (return loss) 20.16 dB over 2.0–2.8 GHz.

Where L-sections live: lumped L and C with usable self-resonance run out around 2–6 GHz (depending on parasitic honesty) — your sub-6 GHz phone is full of them; the 10 GHz radar bench is not. Above that, copper geometry is the component → next section.

2.3 Single-stub matching

No components: a line length d, a shunt stub ℓ

Two lengths of 50 Ω copper, printed free on any PCB (printed circuit board). This is the microwave match.

The same two-move program, in admittance (the last element is shunt!):

  1. rotate y along the SWR circle until it crosses the g = 1 circle (it must — twice per half-lap)
  2. read the leftover b; cut the stub to add −b
  3. arrive at y = 1. Done.

Open stub on PCB (a short needs a via — L5's parasitics); short stub in coax/waveguide.

2.4 Loose ends the chart ties

λ/4 rejoined · forbidden regions

λ/4 revisited: Z₀' = √(Z₀R) needs a real R — a point on the axis. But every SWR circle crosses the axis twice per lap (ours: 27.8 Ω at 0.097 λ, 89.9 Ω at 0.347 λ). Pre-rotate, then transform — line + λ/4 is a two-move program too. Homework 2 built it; now the geometry is yours.

Forbidden regions: a series-first L-section leaves the load along its constant-r circle — if that circle never meets g = 1 (exactly when RL > Z₀), the topology is dead for that load. Same argument kills shunt-first for RL < Z₀, and maps the reach of any fixed tuner. L11 uses this to pick networks that can reach a transistor's awkward Γopt.

2.4 The price

Every exact match is a resonance — Q counts the stored energy

  • L-section: two small elements, Q = 0.62.
  • Stub designs: energy stored in line length — electrical angle 2πd·f/f₀ drifts in proportion to d. Sol 1's 0.91 λ unwinds ~3× faster than sol 2's 0.28 λ.
  • Predicted worst-in-band RL ranking (hour 3 measures): L-section > stub 2 > stub 1 — and stub 1 goes worse than the bare antenna at the band edges.

Bode–Fano (L6), spoken casually: match depth × bandwidth is a budget set by the load's own Q. Networks don't win it; they spend it.

War story: an IoT (Internet of Things) product's antenna matched to 28 dB dead-center of channel 7 — failed regulatory on channels 1 and 13. The fix that shipped: a worse center match (14 dB) holding 12 dB across the whole band. The golden sample was a resonance; the product was a bandwidth. The homework makes you do this trade with numbers.
