The Insertion-Loss Method

Reading: Steer, Microwave and RF Design Vol. 4, ch. 2 (free) · Pozar ch. 8 · Cohn 1957

1.1 The receiver's front door

The course radar's IF strip has neighbors

Lecture 1's radar mixes 10 GHz echoes to a 60 MHz IF (intermediate frequency). Before the 1 MHz detection bandwidth means anything, a bandpass filter must kill both neighbors — without touching the band. That filter is this lecture (and the homework).

VHF = very high frequency (30–300 MHz). Why the IF is 60 MHz: lecture 12's frequency planning.

1.1 Spec language

Three numbers rule every filter conversation

  • Passband ripple — worst attenuation in the band you pass.
    ≤ 0.5 dB, 55–65 MHz
  • Stopband rejection — least attenuation where you must kill.
    ≥ 40 dB at 35 & 85 MHz
  • Group delay τg = −dφ/dω — the phase spec amplitude plots hide (hour 2).

The spec is a mask: the curve must stay above the black bar and below the red ones. The homework's --check prints this table with measured margins — the curve is never the deliverable, the numbers are.

1.1 Why this lecture exists

Three claims for today

  1. Every ladder filter is one filter.
    Design the lowpass prototype once; scaling + transformation reach every f₀, bandwidth, impedance.
  2. Ripple is a currency.
    Letting the passband wobble buys stopband. Measured today: 11.7 dB at equal order — or one whole section.
  3. The bandpass center is the geometric mean √(f₁f₂).
    Not (f₁+f₂)/2. Hour 3 builds the wrong one and watches it fail politely.
1.2 The insertion-loss method

Specify the mathematics, then synthesize the circuit

PLR = (available power)/(delivered power) = 1/|S21|²  ·  IL = 10·log₁₀ PLR

PLR = power loss ratio · IL = insertion loss · S21 = transmission (scattering parameters, L4)

Physical two-port ⇒ PLR = 1 + M(ω²)/N(ω²), a ratio of polynomials. Choose the polynomial → get the response. Extract the ladder → get the filter.

1 · prototype

Ωc = 1, 1 Ω ends, values g₁…gN+1

2 · scale

to 50 Ω

3 · transform

LP → HP / BP / BS at real f

4 · sweep

measure the spec table

LP/HP/BP/BS = lowpass / highpass / bandpass / bandstop. Stage 4 is the one everyone skips — and hour 3's bug survives exactly until stage 4.

1.2 The lowpass prototype

One ladder, N numbers, plus a load

Cutoff Ω = 1 rad/s, source ≈ 1 Ω. Series inductors g₁, g₃, … alternate with shunt capacitors g₂, g₄, … (or the exact dual — same numbers, swapped roles). All shape decisions live in the g-numbers; hours of design collapse into choosing a polynomial.

1.3 Butterworth, derivation 1

Maximally flat — hoard the error at the edge

PLR = 1 + Ω2N
  • At Ω = 0: perfect. First 2N−1 derivatives of PLR vanish — maximally flat.
  • At Ω = 1: always 3.01 dB — the Butterworth band edge is its 3-dB point, by definition. (Remember this; it ambushes a design in hour 2.)
  • Beyond: 20N dB/decade. N = order = element count.

The flatness is bought by spending nothing anywhere else: all the approximation error piles up at the band edge — precisely where your spec was loosest. Chebyshev will call this a waste.

1.3 Butterworth, derivation 2

The poles sit on a circle — and scipy knows it

On the axis, PLR = 1 + (−s²)N: poles of |S21|² solve (−s²)N = −1 — 2N points, equally spaced, unit circle. Keep the left-half N (stability decides).

gk = 2·sin[(2k−1)π/2N]  ·  gN+1 = 1

Sanity anchor N = 1: shunt C across 1 Ω ends → |S21|² = 1/(1+(ωC/2)²); 3 dB at ω = 1 → C = 2 = 2·sin(π/2). Formula meets circuit on the case you can do by hand.
N = 3: [1, 2, 1, 1] — memorize it, it is the canary. N = 4: 0.7654, 1.8478, 1.8478, 0.7654.

1.4 Chebyshev

Equal ripple: spend the whole error budget, everywhere

PLR = 1 + ε²·CN²(Ω)

ε² = 10ripple/10 − 1 (0.5 dB → ε = 0.3493) · CN = Chebyshev polynomial: |CN| ≤ 1 in band, cosh(N·arccosh Ω) outside · CN+1 = 2Ω·CN − CN−1

  • In band: N wiggles between 0 and the ripple; band edge at the ripple (N odd).
  • Outside: CN ≈ ½(2Ω)N — the skirt runs ~6(N−1) dB below Butterworth's, same order.

"More allowed ripple = worse filter"? No — it is a trade. Ripple is in-band error and rejection per section. Measured in hour 3: same order, same 0.5-dB edge, the equal-ripple skirt is 40.52 dB down where maximally-flat manages 28.84 dB. Ripple bought 11.7 dB. Minimax approximation, LC costume.

1.4 The g-value recursion

The homework's core — any N, not a table

β = ln[coth(rippledB/17.37)]  ·  γ = sinh(β/2N)
ak = sin[(2k−1)π/2N]  ·  bk = γ² + sin²(kπ/N)
g₁ = 2a₁/γ  ·  gk = 4ak−1ak / (bk−1·gk−1)
gN+1 = 1 (N odd)  ·  coth²(β/4) (N even)

  • 17.37 = 40/ln 10 — dB of ripple → nepers for the hyperbolics. Nothing mystical.
  • Classic row you will reproduce tonight: 0.5 dB, N = 3 → 1.5963, 1.0967, 1.5963, 1.0.
  • Why a recursion and not the book's table: the checker asks for orders no table prints — and lecture 9 imports this same engine.
1.4 Where the recursion comes from

Earned twice: by hand at N = 1, by machine for all N

Fast (N = 1, by hand): one shunt C between 1 Ω ends; demand exactly the ripple at ω = 1 → C = 2ε. The recursion says g₁ = 2/sinh(β/2) — a two-line hyperbolic identity shows 2/sinh(β/2) = 2ε identically. β and γ are ε in disguise. Hour 3 prints both: 0.698623 = 0.698623.

First-principles (any N): from |S11|² = 1 − 1/PLR, spectral-factorize, form Zin = (1+S11)/(1−S11), and peel the ladder off by continued-fraction division — series L, shunt C, series L, … Each quotient is a g. The homework's referee runs exactly this extraction on scipy's poles and must agree with your recursion to 1e-8. (It agrees to 1e-12.)

S11 = reflection (L4). The symbolic grind to the closed recursion is Takahasi's; we let the machine hold the pencil — and use the agreement as the referee principle: two unrelated paths, one answer.

1.4 The even-N wrinkle

Why gN+1 = 1.9841 is not a typo

  • Even N: CN(0) = ±1 → the response must sit 0.5 dB down at DC (DC = direct current, ω = 0).
  • But a lowpass LC ladder is transparent at DC — series Ls short, shunt Cs open. DC loss can only come from a resistive mismatch.
  • Hence unequal terminations: gN+1 = coth²(β/4) = 1.9841 for 0.5 dB. Check: mismatch loss of 1.9841 vs 1 = −10·log₁₀(1−Γ²) = 0.4999 dB — exactly the ripple.

Nothing in a filter table is arbitrary. Practical corollary: 50 Ω-in/50 Ω-out catalogs are full of odd-order filters. Homework Q3 makes you own this argument.

1.5 Recap

Hour 1 in three sentences

  • The insertion-loss method: choose PLR(Ω) as mathematics, then synthesize the unique ladder that delivers it between real terminations.
  • Butterworth hoards flatness at DC and pays at the skirt; Chebyshev spends the ripple budget everywhere and collects 11.7 dB.
  • The recursion (β, γ, ak, bk) is scipy-checkable — two unrelated paths, one answer is how you will know yours is right.

Hour 2

Scaling, Transformation,
Order, and Delay

1 Ω → 50 Ω · prototype → 60 MHz bandpass (the geometric-mean trap) · the nomogram retired · what steepness costs in phase

2.1 Impedance & frequency scaling

Two substitutions, no new ideas

Impedance × R₀: every impedance scales; no ratio changes, S-parameters (re-referenced) untouched.
L' = R₀L  ·  C' = C/R₀  ·  R' = R₀R

Frequency ÷ ωc: Ω ← ω/ωc.
Combined lowpass recipe:
L' = gR₀/ωc  ·  C' = g/(R₀ωc)

Calibrate hands — 0.5 dB Chebyshev N = 3 lowpass, 100 MHz, 50 Ω:

L₁ = 1.5963·50/2π·10⁸127 nH
C₂ = 1.0967/(50·2π·10⁸)34.9 pF

Buyable parts. A synthesis that ends in numbers you cannot solder is not finished — the checker prints nH and pF for the same reason.

2.2 Lowpass → bandpass

The substitution — and why the center is geometric

Ω ← (1/Δ)·(ω/ω₀ − ω₀/ω)    with   ω₀ = √(ω₁ω₂),   Δ = (ω₂−ω₁)/ω₀

Ω = prototype frequency · ω = real frequency · Δ = fractional bandwidth · ω₁, ω₂ = passband edges

  • ω₀ → Ω = 0 (center ← DC) · ω₁, ω₂ → Ω = ∓1 — only if ω₀ is the geometric mean.
  • Exact symmetry: Ω(ω₀²/ω) = −Ω(ω). Frequencies pair by ratio, not difference — the response is symmetric on a log axis.
  • Ours: f₀ = √(55·65) = 59.7913 MHz, Δ = 0.1672. Not 60.

The 0.35% gap between √(f₁f₂) and (f₁+f₂)/2 = 60 MHz is hour 3's deliberate bug — and it is exactly one ripple-band's width of wrong.

2.2 Elements become resonators

Every g becomes an L-C pair tuned to f₀

Lecture 6's resonators, one per g, all tuned to the same f₀, coupled through the ladder. Cohn 1957 rebuilt filters as "resonators + couplings" — that picture is lecture 9's starting point.

2.2 The traps, named

What the transformation does not forgive

1 · Element spread. Series L over shunt L = 1270/20.3 ≈ 63× — and it grows as g₁g₂/Δ². Narrow the band 3× → spread ~9×. At Δ of a few percent the parts leave the catalog: this, not taste, is why narrowband filters go to coupled resonators (L9).

2 · The center. Any branch resonating off √(f₁f₂) slides the whole ripple band. Hour 3 builds the 60.00 MHz version: rejections still pass, the 55 MHz edge reads 0.97 dB where 0.5 was promised.

Pre-empted objection: "my edges 55/65 aren't geometrically placed around anything nice — is the map still exact?" Yes: f₀ is defined from your edges; the edges always land on Ω = ±1. What is NOT symmetric afterwards is everything else — like a spec written "±25 MHz". Next slide.

2.3 Order estimation

The nomogram, retired by two closed forms

Map the stop frequencies first — the spec's arithmetic symmetry dies in the transform:

Ω(35 MHz) = −6.714  ·  Ω(85 MHz) = +4.294  →  the smaller |Ω| binds: 85 MHz decides

A = (10L_stop/10 − 1)/(10L_edge/10 − 1) = 81,945
Chebyshev: N ≥ arccosh(√A)/arccosh(Ωs) = 2.972 → N = 3
Butterworth: N ≥ log₁₀(A)/(2·log₁₀ Ωs) = 3.882 → N = 4

The Butterworth fine print: its "band edge" must be the 0.5-dB point (our promise), not the traditional 3-dB point — build A with L_edge = 0.5 dB, or widen the design band by ε−1/N. Use the 3-dB habit and you ship a filter that is 3 dB down at 65 MHz: legal Butterworth, failed spec.

Claim 2, settled: 3 sections of Chebyshev = 4 of Butterworth for this spec. Homework Q1 asks you to predict this gap before the checker confirms it.

2.4 Group delay

Where the steep filter pays

τg = −dφ/dω

φ = phase of S21 · flat τg in band = distortionless

  • Delay peaks at the band edges — edge poles sit nearest the jω axis; steeper amplitude ⇒ higher, sharper peaks.
  • Measured (both filters at the same 0.5-dB edges):
    Chebyshev N=3: 68.3 ns center, 128.5 ns worst edge
    Butterworth N=4: 63.9 ns center, 99.2 ns edge — flatter despite the extra section.

Radar tie-in: 60 ns of in-band delay spread = c·Δτ/2 ≈ 9 m of range smear on the echo's pulse edge (L15 pays for this). The steepest filter that meets the rejection spec is not automatically the right filter. (Delay-first designs: Bessel — named today, used when a pulse comes out smeared.)

2.5 Where lumped stops working

The same recipe at 2.4 GHz — parts smaller than their parasitics

60 MHz (this week — shelf parts):

series1270 nH / 5.58 pF
shunt20.3 nH / 349 pF

2.4 GHz, 10% bandwidth (hour 3 prints it):

series52.9 nH / 0.083 pF
shunt0.30 nH / 14.5 pF
  • 0.083 pF < the parasitic of its own solder pad.
  • 0.30 nH < one via's inductance.
  • A real 53 nH coil self-resonates below 2.4 GHz — up there it is a capacitor.

What survives: the spec language, the g-values, the transformations, the order formulas. Lecture 9 keeps the mathematics, swaps the hardware — Richards' transformation, Kuroda's identities, coupled-line filters. Same brain, new copper.

2.5 Recap

Hour 2 in three sentences

  • Bandpass = every g becomes a resonator at √(f₁f₂) — geometric, and the checker measures every branch.
  • Order comes from the closed forms after mapping the binding stop edge: 85 MHz → Ω = 4.294 → Chebyshev 3, Butterworth 4.
  • Group delay peaks at the edges and prices the steepness: 60 ns spread ≈ 9 m of radar smear.
