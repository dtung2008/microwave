1.1 Why frequency conversion exists

Your LNA hands you 10 GHz. Now what?

Gain

100 dB at 10 GHz: mW per dB, and lecture 11's stability nightmares through every stray coupling

Filtering

a 10 MHz channel at 10 GHz is 0.1% fractional bandwidth — lecture 8's order formula laughs

Digitizing

a 10 GHz ADC (analog-to-digital converter) is a research budget; 100 MS/s (megasamples per second) is lunch money

Gain, filtering, and ADCs live at low frequency. So we move the signal — without disturbing the information riding on it. The mixer does the moving; the LNA (low-noise amplifier, L11) guards the noise figure while it happens.

"Why not sample at RF (radio frequency) directly?" — you can (below ~6 GHz); the sampler is itself a mixer, and every problem on this slide relocates instead of leaving. Slide 15.

1.1 Why this lecture exists

Three claims for today

  1. Mixing is multiplication — and multiplication needs nonlinearity.
    A linear circuit can never create a new frequency. Every mixer is a nonlinearity or a switch in a suit.
  2. The mixer is a perfect mirror.
    Two RF frequencies land on one IF, indistinguishably. The only defenses act before the mixer.
  3. The oscillator's skirt is a system parameter.
    Phase noise, in dBc/Hz, decides how slow a drone your Doppler radar can see. Today: that number is 2.54 m/s.

IF = intermediate frequency, the fixed low frequency the receiver converts to · dBc/Hz = dB relative to the carrier, per hertz. Both get their own slides.

1.2 Mixing is multiplication

One line of trig runs the receiver industry

cos(ω₁t)·cos(ω₂t) = ½ cos((ω₁−ω₂)t) + ½ cos((ω₁+ω₂)t)

RF × LO (local oscillator) → a copy at the difference — the IF (intermediate frequency) — and a copy at the sum, filtered off.

The ½ is a price: the kept sideband carries power ¼ — an ideal multiplier converts at −6.02 dB. Amplitude and phase of the RF ride through untouched: the information moved; nothing about it changed. Hour 3 measures both claims by FFT.

Claim 1, formally: sinusoids are eigenfunctions of linear time-invariant systems (lecture 2's phasor bargain). New frequencies require multiplication — nonlinearity or switching. There is no third way.

1.3 The diode detector

The square-law detector — the mixer's ancestor

The diode, Taylor-expanded (lecture 10):

i ≈ a₁v + a₂v² + a₃v³ + …

Feed a₂v² a modulated carrier v = A(t)·cos ωt:

a₂A²(t)/2 · (1 + cos 2ωt)

baseband term ∝ A² — the envelope, carrier discarded; the 2ω term goes to the capacitor.

Square law = output ∝ input power — true while the signal is small (≲ −20 dBm for a typical Schottky); driven harder it slides into linear peak detection.

This is the crystal radio and the diode power sensor: no LO, no tuning — and no selectivity, brutal sensitivity. Lecture 1's radar does not hunt drones with a crystal radio. Give the same diode an LO, though…

1.3 The single-diode mixer

Pump the diode with an LO — and inherit the whole grid

With v = vRF + vLO, the a₂v² term contains

2a₂ · vRF · vLO

— slide 4's multiplication, manufactured by a nonlinearity. That cross-term is the IF.

But a₃, a₄, … make everything else too. The output contains every |m·fLO ± n·fRF|. The grid is not a footnote — it is homework module 1.

Hour 3, measured (behavioral diode, exp(v), audio rate): all 24 products to order 3 land in their exact predicted FFT bins — (2,2) at −31.9 dBc, (3,3) at −62.8 dBc, and 4·fLO at −41.8 dBc beyond the grid's edge.

Every product is a door an interferer can walk through. Frequency planning is deciding, in advance, that nobody strong lives behind any of them.

1.3 Real mixers are switches

The Fourier view — and the 3.92 dB floor

An LO-pumped diode is ON half the cycle, OFF the other: the RF is multiplied by a ±1 square wave,

sq(t) = (4/π)[cos ωLOt − ⅓cos 3ωLOt + ⅕cos 5ωLOt − …]

Products at (odd m)·fLO ± fRF; the wanted line gets amplitude 2/π.

Ideal switching conversion loss: 20·log₁₀(π/2) = 3.92 dB. Real diode mixers: 6–8 dB (series resistance, mismatch, re-converted products). Hour 3 measures −3.92 exactly.

Why datasheets shout about LO drive (+7/+13/+17 dBm): the LO is the switch's power supply. A starved switch multiplies badly — loss rises, the grid blooms. Balance (double-balanced rings) cancels even-order products and leak-through ~50–60 dB by symmetry — but the image is order (1,1). Balance cleans the grid; it does not un-mirror the mirror.

1.4 The image frequency

The mirror: two frequencies, one IF

The receiver keeps |fLO − fRF| = fIF. Solve for fRF: two solutions —

fRF = fLO + fIF   or   fLO − fIF

Both exactly IF from the LO, one per side, equal conversion gain. The unwanted one is the image — 2·IF away from the signal.

Hour 3, by construction: signal alone → IF bin amp 0.50; image alone → same bin, 0.25; both → 0.75. One bin, two owners, inseparable after the mixer. The defense was before the mixer or nowhere.

1.4 The image frequency

What the mirror costs — and the shuttle-bus radar

  • Noise folds too: an unfiltered image adds up to 3 dB of noise figure — the DSB vs SSB (double- vs single-sideband) NF split on every mixer datasheet.
  • High-side LO inverts the IF spectrum (the fold reverses frequency order): a positive Doppler arrives as a negative IF offset. Your processing must know the LO side — or every velocity has the wrong sign (L15).

War story: campus perimeter radar, S-band, 2.9 GHz, low-side LO 2.65 GHz, IF 250 MHz. Commissioning week it detected every airport shuttle bus at extraordinary range — and only shuttle buses. 2.9 − 2×0.25 = 2.4 GHz: the image band was the parking lot's Wi-Fi; every bus carried an access point. Nothing was broken. The radar worked perfectly — as a Wi-Fi detector. The fix was a frequency plan, not a repair.

"Can't I just filter the image?" — yes, if it is far enough away to filter. The image sits 2·IF out, so the IF choice sets the filter difficulty. Hour 2 opens there.

1.5 Recap

Hour 1 in four sentences

  • Conversion exists because gain, filtering, and ADCs live low.
  • Mixing is multiplication; nonlinearity or switching supplies it — with the whole m·fLO ± n·fRF grid attached, and a 3.92 dB switching floor.
  • The image is a perfect mirror at 2·IF, folded noise included; nothing downstream unfolds it.
  • A radar once detected shuttle buses because nobody asked who transmits in the image band.

2.1 The superheterodyne

Armstrong 1918: tune the LO, fix everything else

Convert the tunable RF problem to a fixed IF where gain is cheap, filters are sharp, and the ADC is happy. Tune by moving the LO, never the filters. A century old; still the default.

DSP = digital signal processing — lectures 14–16 live in that last box.

2.1 Frequency planning

Choosing the IF is a two-sided squeeze

Image rejection wants IF high. The image sits 2·IF out. Our preselector must pass all of 10.0–10.4 GHz — so if 2·IF < 400 MHz the image lands inside our own band. No filter order fixes that.
Hard rule: IF > 200 MHz — architectural, not parametric.

Everything else wants IF low. Low-frequency filters are low-order (lecture 8's Ωs grows with offset/f₀); IF gain is cheap; the ADC's analog input bandwidth (500 MHz) caps the ride.

Candidate IF = 60 MHz (lecture 8's classic): image 120 MHz out — inside the tuning band. Dead on arrival, by architecture. Candidate IF = 321.4 MHz (a real catalog filter): image 642.8 MHz out — plannable. Which side of the LO? The emitter table votes next.

2.1 Frequency planning · the ADC

The sampler is a mixer too — Nyquist zones

Sampling at fs folds the axis at every multiple of fs/2. Each interval [k·fs/2, (k+1)·fs/2] is a Nyquist zone; a band that fits one zone lands intact at baseband (order-reversed in odd zones).

321.4 MHz ± 5 MHz sits mid-zone-6 of a 100 MS/s converter → undersampling, standard practice. The IF filter doubles as the anti-alias filter. Real ADC constraints: zone fit + 500 MHz input bandwidth. (Hour 3 already watched a line alias, live.)

The plan's filter bill, in lecture 8's language (Chebyshev, 0.5 dB ripple, 60 dB):

filterjoborder
preselector, 10.2 GHz60 dB at 10.643n = 7
IF, 321.4 MHz60 dB at zone edgesn = 4

Two numbers that are the sentence "filtering lives low" — and the IF filter is doing the harder fractional job (Q4).

2.2 Direct conversion — one honest slide

Set IF = 0: no image band, new demons

DC offset

the LO leaks into its own mixer and self-mixes to a rock-solid error at DC (direct current) — on your signal

Flicker

the transistors' 1/f corner sits mid-channel

IQ imbalance

the in-phase/quadrature mixer pair never quite matches; the constellation smears

Even-order IMD

a₂ intermodulation lands at DC here (harmless in a superhet) — hence the IIP2 spec (input second-order intercept, lecture 10's IP3 sibling)

Fixable with per-chip calibration and DSP at smartphone volumes — every phone does it. But a radar hunting slow things near zero Doppler should not park its most precious octave on top of its own DC offset and flicker corner. The course radar stays superhet.

2.3 Oscillators & phase noise

Reading the skirt: L(f) in dBc/Hz

A real oscillator (VCO — voltage-controlled oscillator — plus PLL, phase-locked loop) is cos(2πf₀t + φ(t)). The phase-noise spectrum is quoted as L(f), dBc/Hz: dB relative to the carrier, per hertz, at offset f.

The course LO, read aloud: −40 flat inside the 10 Hz PLL loop · −30 dB/dec to −70 at 100 Hz (1/f³) · −20 dB/dec: −90 at 1 kHz, −110 at 10 kHz. Integrated: 3.13° RMS — a superb number. Hold that thought for slide 19.

2.3 Oscillators & phase noise

Leeson, qualitatively — where skirts come from

An oscillator is an amplifier whose noise circulates through a resonator forever. Inside the resonator's half-bandwidth f₀/2Q the loop integrates phase kicks → 1/f² skirt (device flicker stacks 1/f³ close in); outside, the amplifier's plain floor.

  • Three levers, only: resonator Q (lecture 6), power through the loop, device flicker.
  • A 10 GHz microstrip-resonator VCO (Q ≈ 100) wears a wide skirt; sapphire or multiplied-crystal sources wear narrow ones.
  • "Just buy a cleaner oscillator" = "buy a better resonator" — there is no third lever to pull.

Full Leeson derivation: a second course. What a system engineer needs is the plot on slide 17 and the arithmetic on slide 20.

2.3 Phase noise at system level

The skirt stands on the slow drone

Every downconverted echo wears the LO's skirt. Usually harmless — 70 dB down. But clutter (the ground) is +60 dB over the drone (L14's number), parked at zero Doppler, wearing the full skirt.

The drone's line sits at fd = 2v/λ ≈ 68 Hz per m/s (10.2 GHz). A slow drone must out-shout the clutter's skirt at its own offset — not the thermal floor.

Hour 3, measured: at 60 dB clutter, the 3 m/s drone clears the skirt by 14.4 dB; the 1 m/s drone shows 2.5 dB — buried. Same RCS (radar cross section), same range, same power. You can lose a target to a component that consumed zero dB of the link budget.

2.3 Phase noise at system level

The minimum visible speed, in four lines

Visible ⇔ line clears local skirt by 13 dB in the Doppler bin (ENBW — equivalent noise bandwidth — 1.5 Hz for a 1 s Hann frame):

L(fd) ≤ −60 − 13 − 10log₁₀(1.5) = −74.8 dBc/Hz

Read the profile backwards (−20 dB/dec segment): fd = 100·104.76/20 = 173 Hz → vmin = 173/68 = 2.54 m/s.

And the slope is the price list: in the −20 dB/dec region, every halving of speed costs 6.02 dB of margin (9 dB per halving below 100 Hz). Homework Q2 makes you predict this before running. Fine print: TX and LO share the oscillator, so close-in clutter partially cancels (range correlation) — our number is the honest long-range worst case.

2.4 The FMCW dechirp receiver

The mixer is the ranging engine

FMCW (frequency-modulated continuous wave): transmit a ramp, B hertz in Tc seconds, slope α = B/Tc — and mix the echo with the transmit chirp itself. The LO is the waveform.

fb = α·τ = 2Rα/c

A constant delay became a constant beat tone: range is now a frequency, and the receiver is a mixer plus an FFT. Full derivation (and the range–Doppler coupling fine print): lecture 15.
