1.1 The job

A client email arrives

"We need our 12.5 Ω power-amplifier termination matched to the 50 Ω world — 20 dB return loss, 2 to 4 GHz, full octave. Also attaching three resonator sweeps from our bench; please report their Qs. Timeline is tight."

4 : 1

50 → 12.5 Ω impedance ratio

20 dB

RL (return loss) = −20·log₁₀|Γ| → |Γ| ≤ 0.1

66.7%

fractional bandwidth (an octave: Δf/f₀ = 2/3)

3 × Q

three swept resonators to qualify (Q = quality factor)

Three hidden questions: is the spec physical? (hour 2) · design the match (hour 1) · read the resonator sweeps honestly (hour 2).

1.1 Why this lecture exists

Three claims for today

  1. Bandwidth is bought with sections.
    Split one big impedance step into N small ones; split cleverly and each section buys ~11.4 dB.
  2. There is a ceiling, and it is a theorem.
    Bode–Fano prices the best any network can do — from the load alone. Feasibility before design.
  3. A resonator's measured Q is not its Q.
    The 3-dB width gives the loaded Q; the coupling hides the real one. This week's correction: ×25.
1.1 The single-section baseline

One λ/4 section is a 17% device on a 67% job

Lecture 2's fix: Z₁ = √(Z₀ZL) = √(50·12.5) = 25 Ω, a quarter wave at f₀ = 3 GHz.

  • At f₀: perfect, by construction.
  • At the band edges (2, 4 GHz): |Γ| = 0.351 → 9.09 dB against a 20 dB spec.
  • Measured 20-dB bandwidth: 17.1%. The client wants 66.7%.

The match is a resonance (lecture 3's warning, now priced). Widening it = adding sections. Choosing the split = the rest of this hour.

1.2 Theory of small reflections

N sections, N+1 junctions, N+1 partial reflections

Γk = (Zk+1 − Zk)/(Zk+1 + Zk)   ·   θ = βℓ = electrical length of one section

The whole design freedom of this hour is the choice of the Γk — which is the choice of the impedance steps.

1.2 Theory of small reflections

Keep the first bounce, drop the rattles

Γ(θ) ≈ Γ₀ + Γ₁e−2jθ + Γ₂e−4jθ + … + ΓNe−2jNθ

Symmetric designs (Γk = ΓN−k) collapse to a cosine series:
Γ(θ) = 2e−jNθ[Γ₀cos Nθ + Γ₁cos(N−2)θ + …] (even-N middle term counts once)

What got dropped: every multiple-bounce product ΓiΓj… — a first-order theory whose error is set by the total ratio, not by N. For ZL/Z₀ = 4 the total is ½·ln 4 = 0.69 — not small. This theory will design our transformer, then lie about its ripple. Measurably. (Slide 13.)

1.3 Binomial — maximally flat

All zeros at band center

Γ(θ) = A(1 + e−2jθ)N  →  |Γ| = 2N|A||cos θ|N,   A = 2−(N+1)ln(ZL/Z₀)

Design rule — each step takes a binomial coefficient's share of the log ratio:
ln(Zk+1/Zk) = 2−N·CNk·ln(ZL/Z₀)

Our job, worked:

The binomial's personality: superb at f₀, spendthrift at the edges — its polynomial zeros are all stacked where we needed exactly one. A "20 dB everywhere" spec rewards holding the worst point. That is an equal-ripple job →

1.4 Chebyshev — equal ripple

The polynomial that ripples, then explodes

TN(cos φ) = cos Nφ  (inside |x| ≤ 1)
TN(x) = cosh(N·arccosh x)  (outside)

Recursion for the table: TN+1 = 2x·TN − TN−1; at x = 2:  2, 7, 26, 97, 362…

Substitute x = sec θm·cos θ: in-band the argument stays inside [−1,1] → ripple ≤ 1; out of band it explodes. θm = band-edge electrical length.

1.4 Chebyshev — equal ripple

The design statement — and this octave's clean number

Γ(θ) = Γm·e−jNθ·TN(sec θm·cos θ)   with   Γm·TN(sec θm) = ½|ln(ZL/Z₀)|

Γm = in-band ripple (the worst |Γ| the design ever shows in band)

For the client's octave: θm = (π/2)(f₁/f₀) = 60°, so sec θm = 2. Given N, the ripple is fixed: Γm = ½|ln(1/4)| / TN(2) = 0.693/TN(2)

Same equation read backwards = bandwidth from ripple: pick Γm, solve TN(sec θm) for θm. One formula, both directions of the trade.

1.4 Chebyshev — the recursion

From ripple to copper: N=2 worked in full

Expansion identities (N ≤ 3 — homework accepts these or a numeric projection):
T₂(s·cos θ) = s²(1 + cos 2θ) − 1  ·  T₃(s·cos θ) = s³(cos 3θ + 3cos θ) − 3s·cos θ   (s = sec θm)

Match to 2[Γ₀cos 2θ + ½Γ₁] term by term (middle term once!):

  • 2Γ₀ = Γm·sec²θm → Γ₀ = 0.198
  • Γ₁ = Γm(sec²θm − 1) → Γ₁ = 0.297
  • ln(Zk+1/Zk) = 2Γk, signs DOWN for 50 → 12.5
Z₁ = 50·e−0.396 = 33.65 Ω,  Z₂ = 18.57 Ω

Endpoint check: 18.57·e−0.396 = 12.50 ✓

The invariant that survives every slip:
2·ΣΓk = ln(ZL/Z₀) = −1.386, exactly.
And symmetry: Γk = ΓN−k.

N=3 by the same recipe: [40.40, 25.00, 15.47] Ω — odd-N middle section is always √(Z₀ZL) = 25.

1.5 Tapered lines — in one picture

N → ∞: the staircase becomes a taper

Γ ≈ ½∫e−2jβz·d(ln Z)/dz·dz

|Γ(f)| is the Fourier spectrum of the impedance slope — window-function intuition, three lectures before lecture 13 needs it.

  • exponential: constant slope — simple, high sidelobes
  • triangular: smooth ends — lower sidelobes, longer
  • Klopfenstein: the Chebyshev limit — shortest for a given ripple

The taper's virtue: above its cutoff it never stops working — a high-pass match (multisections are band-pass). Its price: length — ~λ/2 at the lowest frequency vs three λ/4 at f₀. Mathematics: Pozar §5.8, assigned, not lectured.

2.1 The Bode–Fano criterion

The load issues a budget — spendable anywhere, capped

∫₀∞ ln(1/|Γ(ω)|) dω ≤ π/(RC)

for a parallel R-C load (R = 12.5 Ω here, C = the pad capacitance); ln(1/|Γ|) = "match quality" — large where the match is good

Spend it flat over Δω, nothing outside (|Γ| = 1):
Δω·ln(1/Γm) ≤ π/(RC)
band × depth ≤ budget — every factor is one word of the client's email

Derivation: analyticity of ln|Γ(s)| + the energy the capacitor must store — outline in the script; full account Pozar §5.9 / Fano 1950.

Fine print: the bound assumes infinitely many lossless elements and a brick-wall |Γ|. It separates impossible from conceivable — it never promises achievable. (78.96 dB is not a design target.)

2.1 The career skill

"Your spec violates a theorem" — the three exits

Narrow Δω

10 pF supports 20 dB over at most 1.74 GHz — offer 2.3–4.0 GHz, not the octave

Relax Γm

the full octave at 10 pF caps at 17.37 dB — offer 17 dB (honestly: 15, for margin)

Fix the load

respin the pad below 8.686 pF — the client's actual choice: 2.2 pF

One exit per factor of Δω·ln(1/Γm) ≤ π/RC. Homework Q3 is this email, written with your own module's numbers.

And the answer to "where was the ceiling in hour 1?" — the transformer's load was pure resistance: C = 0, no stored energy, no ceiling. Bode–Fano bites loads that store energy.

2.2 Resonators

Near resonance, everything is an RLC circuit

Series R-L-C (R = loss, L·C set ω₀ = 1/√(LC)):

Zin = R + jωL + 1/(jωC)
≈ R(1 + j·2Qu·Δω/ω₀)  near ω₀

Δω = ω − ω₀ (detuning). Parallel circuit: the exact dual, Yin ≈ G(1 + j2QuΔω/ω₀).

Why this matters: every single-mode resonator — microstrip, coax, cavity — is one of these two near its resonance. One algebra, one extraction method, any hardware. (That is why the homework dares to hand you three wildly different resonators.)

Which dual? Impedance minimum at resonance → series-type. Impedance maximum → parallel-type. Ask the hardware, not the schematic.

2.2 Q, derived twice

Q (quality factor): energy bookkeeping = pole location

Level 1 — bookkeeping:
Q = ω₀·(energy stored)/(power dissipated)
series: Qu = ω₀L/R = 1/(ω₀RC)

Level 2 — the pole: natural response s = −α ± jωd; ring it and energy decays e−ω₀t/Q. Qu = ω₀/2α — radians of ringing until 1/e.

Fourier-transform the decaying ring → a Lorentzian line whose half-power full width is Δω = ω₀/Q.
The 3-dB method just reads the pole's real part off a spectrum. Every measurement trap in 2.4 is a way of corrupting this sentence.

2.3 Transmission-line resonators

Lecture 2's standing waves, kept on purpose

  • shorted λ/2: series-type (impedance minimum)
  • open λ/2: parallel-type (impedance maximum)
  • shorted λ/4: parallel-type — half the copper, free heat-sink anchor; the combline workhorse

Mapping recipe every time: expand Zin(ω₀+Δω) via the tangent transformation, pattern-match against R(1 + j2QΔω/ω₀).

From the expansion of a low-loss shorted λ/2 line (Zin = Z₀(αℓ + jπΔω/ω₀)):
Qu = β/(2α) — stored wave per lost wave.
α = loss (nepers/m), β = phase constant (rad/m) — lecture 2's γ = α + jβ.

It prices technologies instantly — the Q ladder on the previous slide is just α ladders: tan δ (FR-4) → air coax → silver walls.

2.4 Coupling

You cannot measure Qu directly — measuring means coupling

1/QL = 1/Qu + 1/Qe

Qu

unloaded — the resonator alone, its own losses

Qe

external — the loss your probes/loops/gaps add

QL

loaded — what any sweep actually measures

Coupling coefficient κ = Qu/Qe; critical coupling κ = 1 (all the resonator's loss matched by the port — deepest reflection dip, the oscillator designer's sweet spot).

The probe that lets energy in lets it out. The instrument is part of the circuit.

2.4 Measuring Q — the 3-dB method and its traps

The sweep gives QL; the peak buys back Qu

Transmission-coupled resonator, swept |S21|:

  • 3-dB (half-power) width → QL = f₀/Δf3dB
  • peak transmission → the correction:
Qu = QL / (1 − |S21(f₀)|)

|S21(f₀)| LINEAR — never dB

The trap: strong coupling → |S21(f₀)| → 1 → QL ≪ Qu. A resonator with 0.35 dB insertion loss has |S21| = 0.96: its Qu is 25× the 3-dB number. Report QL as Qu and you slander the cavity by 25×.

War story: the Qu ≥ 8000 cavity failed at "Q = 500" — with loops chosen for good SNR (signal-to-noise ratio). Insertion loss at resonance: 0.4 dB → loading ×20 → the batch was fine. A Q quoted without its coupling is not a measurement.
