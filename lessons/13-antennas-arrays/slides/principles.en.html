1.2 The far-field picture

Far enough away, the angular shape stops changing — that shape is the pattern

Pattern F(θ, φ): power density vs direction, normalized to its own peak. Three features carry the whole day: the main lobe, its −3 dB width — HPBW (half-power beamwidth) — and the sidelobes.

1.2 Directivity and gain

Gain is concentration — never amplification

D = peak density / sphere-average density = 4π/ΩA  ·  G = e·D
  • ΩA = beam solid angle; the isotropic radiator has D = 1, every real antenna concentrates.
  • e = radiation efficiency (ohmic + dielectric loss). Clean radar metalwork: 0.7–0.95 → today G ≈ D unless flagged.
  • Units: dBi = gain over isotropic; dBd = gain over a half-wave dipole (dBd + 2.15 = dBi).

"33 dBi makes the signal 2000× stronger — like an amplifier?" No. Same total watts, shoved into a 4.5° cone instead of a sphere. Point the cone elsewhere and the "gain" is negative. An amplifier is generous; an antenna is merely opinionated — and the opinion works in receive too (why G² sits in the radar equation).

1.2 Polarization

The far field is a vector — the receiver projects onto its own

"orthogonal" in real hardware (−∞ only on paper)

War story: a range test "lost" 3 dB everywhere for a week — every cable swapped, every connector torqued twice. Linear horn, circularly polarized device under test; nobody wrote polarization on the test plan, because polarization is invisible on a spectrum analyzer. The 3 dB was physics, findable only at the whiteboard.

(RHCP = right-hand circular polarization. Radar meteorology and GPS live on this slide; we mostly stay co-polarized.)

1.3 Effective aperture

Ae = Gλ²/4π — Friis closes its loop

Pr = S · Ae,   Ae = G λ² / 4π
  • Why universal: reciprocity — receive and transmit are the same physics run backwards, so Ae/G is one constant for every antenna. Solve any one antenna exactly and λ²/4π falls out (derivation: Orfanidis ch. 15 — assigned, not board work).
  • At 10 GHz the isotropic antenna's aperture is λ²/4π = 0.715 cm² — space hands every antenna a λ-sized catch basin. This is why lecture 1's λ² lived in the aperture, not the medium.
  • Course dish: 33 dBi ↔ Ae = 0.143 m² — a 43 cm circle. The hardware on the roof is about that size. Numbers cohere.

Pr = PtGtGrλ²/(4πR)² was never anything but density × aperture. Every symbol now has an owner.

1.3 One fact, three currencies

Gain ↔ aperture ↔ beamwidth

G = 4πAe/λ²

gain from area

θ ≈ λ/D

beamwidth (rad) from size D

D ≈ 4π/θ²

directivity from beamwidth

Concentrating into θ ≈ λ/D requires an aperture D wide — narrow beams are purchased with physical size, in wavelengths. Hour 2 ends with one division in this currency: what separates two drones 100 m apart at 5 km?

Sanity check en route: 33 dBi → θ ≈ √(4π/1995) ≈ 4.5°. Hold that number; it fails the drone question later.

1.4 Workhorse elements

The microstrip patch — lecture 5's stackup grows a radiator

The resonant-length story: a λ/2-in-dielectric resonator whose two open edges leak, in phase, broadside — an accidental two-element array.

Design formulas (provided): W = (c/2f)√(2/(εr+1)); εeff Hammerstad-style; L = c/(2f√εeff) − 2ΔL with ΔL the fringing extension.

10 GHz on RO4350B (εr = 3.66, h = 0.508 mm): 9.82 mm × 7.68 mm. L = 0.256 λ₀ — shorter than λ/2 because εeff = 3.37 shrinks the wave and fringing (ΔL = 0.24 mm/edge) shrinks the copper.

1.4 Workhorse elements

The horn — and the moral of the element zoo

Calculable to fractions of a dB → the standard-gain horn is what every antenna range measures against.

Elements are 2–8 dBi devices. Nobody builds a 33 dBi element — high gain means big aperture, and big apertures are built by replication, not heroic single radiators.

Which is the cue for hour 2: the team.

2.1 The setup

N elements on a line — one drawing carries every formula today

AF(θ) = Σn wn ejnψ, ψ = kd·sin θ, k = 2π/λ — the array factor (AF), with complex weights wn. That line is the whole theory; a ULA (uniform linear array) is this drawing with equal spacing.

2.1 The array factor, derived twice

Fast: a geometric series · First-principles: phasor spokes

|AF| = |sin(Nψ/2) / sin(ψ/2)|   (uniform weights; peak N at ψ = 0; nulls at ψ = 2πm/N)

Nulls are complete wraps; sidelobes are extra half-wraps. No taper exists yet — this is the raw geometry. (Week 15, "why does my FFT (fast Fourier transform) leak": this picture, ψ renamed.)

2.2 Anatomy of the uniform beam

Course array: N = 16, d = λ/2, 10 GHz — every feature a number

SLL (sidelobe level): sinc limit −13.26 dB; the finite N = 16 array measures −13.15 dB (+0.11 dB — denominator N·sin(ψ/2), not Nψ/2). Both honest: limit vs hardware. And: more elements do NOT lower the sidelobes — only the weight shape does (§2.4).

2.3 Grating lobes

The AF repeats every 2π of ψ — d > λ/2 lets a replica into the sky

Spatial aliasing. Elements sample the wavefront; d > λ/2 is under-sampling; the grating lobe is the alias — same height, wrong direction. (Yes, DSP — digital signal processing — students: exactly Nyquist.)

2.3 The d > λ/2 crime

The spacing budget — and when designers sin anyway

d < λ / (1 + |sin θ₀|)
  • Scan to ±90° → back to d = λ/2. Scan only to ±45° → dmax = 0.586λ = 17.56 mm for the course array.
  • Hour 3 animates d = 0.65λ: onset at scan 32.6°; steered 45°, the lobe stands at −56.24° at full height — the array cannot tell −56° from +45°.

War story: a harbor array, spacing opened past λ/2 to stretch aperture; boresight commissioning flawless. Night shift, weeks later: inbound contact at −55°, nothing on camera — the ferry at +45° was the contact; the array reported its alias. Fix: not software (you cannot filter an alias after sampling) — masts moved 3 mm each.

Deliberate sinners: narrow scan sectors (small θ₀ in the budget), element patterns trusted to sit on the grating angle (§2.5). Automotive radars do it daily. The crime is not d > λ/2 — it is doing it without the budget line.

2.4 Tapers are windows

The array factor IS the DFT of the weights — the DSP homecoming

AF(ψ) = Σn wn ejnψ  =  DFT of w, read at ψ = kd·sin θ
  • Uniform weights = rectangular window = −13 dB leakage. Sidelobes are spectral leakage in space.
  • Hour 3 proves it with one np.fft.fft call: agreement to 4.5×10⁻¹⁴.
  • So shop for windows: raised cosine (Hann): −31.5 dB, but HPBW ×1.53. Chebyshev (Dolph 1946): for a chosen SLL, the narrowest possible main lobe — all sidelobes at exactly the spec, equal ripple (lecture 8's polynomial, same bargain).

One call: scipy.signal.windows.chebwin(16, at=30) — its equal-ripple floor is the homework's second referee.

(DFT = discrete Fourier transform.)

2.4 The taper trade, measured

17 dB of quiet costs 26% of beamwidth and 0.65 dB of gain

Measured: HPBW 6.359° → 7.980° (×1.2550), directivity 12.04 → 11.39 dBi (−0.65 dB), SLL −13.15 → −30.00 exactly. Sidelobes are cheap; beamwidth is precious. The homework's drone (echo −17.8 dB): below a −13 dB floor, 12 dB proud of a −30 dB floor.

2.5 Steering

Steering is linear phase — and it is not free

phase of element n: −kd·n·sin θ₀  →  ψ → kd(sin θ − sin θ₀): the whole anatomy translates to θ₀

Broadening

aperture foreshortens: 24 cm seen from 45° is 17 cm → ×1/cos θ₀. Measured: 6.359° → 9.025° (×1.4194; exact arcsin form beats 1/cos's 1.4142)

Scan loss

same foreshortening shrinks Ae: gain ≈ ×cos θ₀ (−1.5 dB at 45°) — before the element pattern's own droop

Grating exposure

§2.3's budget with sin θ₀ spent — steer wide, shrink d

No motors, microseconds — this is the phased array. Lecture 16 does it to received snapshots in software and calls it beamforming.

2.5 Pattern multiplication

Identical elements ⇒ the total pattern factors

Etotal(θ) = Eelement(θ) × AF(θ)
  • The element is a slow envelope (a patch: broad, ~cos θ); the AF is the fast structure.
  • Why grating-lobe sinners survive: the element envelope multiplies the alias down.
  • Why arrays of arrays work: a row of 16 becomes the "element" of a column of 16 — the planar pattern is the product. One principle, three uses, zero new mathematics.
