Noise & Nonlinearity
The Receiver's Two Ceilings

Principles (100 min) → break → Tools: the cascade engine + two tones, live → homework: the receiver budget

Reading: Friis 1944, "Noise Figures of Radio Receivers" (4 pages, whole) · Steer Vol. 1 ch. 4 + Vol. 5 ch. 4 (free)

1.1 The two ceilings

A receiver is squeezed between two limits

  • Below: the thermal noise floor — weaker signals are simply gone. Lecture 1's −174 dBm/Hz, today with its physics.
  • Above: nonlinearity — stronger signals manufacture false signals (spurs) inside your own passband.
  • Between them: the dynamic range where the receiver actually works. Receiver design = prying the two apart.

Lecture 1 assumed NF (noise figure) = 3 dB and never said how. This week the box is opened — and it contains an ordering problem.

1.1 The two ceilings

Three claims for today

  1. Noise cascades by Friis 1944, and the first stage rules.
    Every stage's noise is forgiven by the gain in front of it.
  2. Nonlinearity is a Taylor series — every spur is algebra.
    IP3 (third-order intercept) is a fiction that predicts real spurs to fractions of a dB.
  3. Ordering, not parts, sets the radar's range.
    Same five blocks, same gain — 12.9 dB of NF spread → ×2.1 in drone detection range.
1.2 Where noise comes from

Every resistor is a transmitter

Pavailable = kTB
  • k = Boltzmann's constant; T = physical temperature (K); B = the bandwidth you listen in.
  • At T₀ = 290 K: −173.98 dBm/Hz — lecture 1's −174, now with its origin.
  • B = 1 MHz → floor at −113.98 dBm.

Johnson measured it (1928); Nyquist explained it — in the very next paper of the same journal issue. Fine print: the full Planck form rolls off near 6 THz at 290 K; at microwave, kTB is exact to parts in 10⁴.

1.2 Where noise comes from · first principles

Available power from a resistor — why the R cancels

  1. Johnson's measurement, Nyquist's equipartition argument: open-circuit noise voltage density v̄² = 4kTR per hertz.
  2. Matched load R: voltage divides, load sees v/2 → power (v̄²/4)/R·B = kTB.
  3. The R cancels. 50 Ω or 1 MΩ — same available noise power.

One number rules all receivers — that is why a single floor (−174 dBm/Hz) appears on every datasheet on earth. And B is a choice: noise is a density.

Shot noise: current is charge in lumps — density 2qI, white; matters in diodes/mixers (L12). Flicker (1/f) noise: below a device's corner frequency — harmless at 10 GHz, lethal near DC after the mixer (L12's direct-conversion demons). Today: thermal rules.

1.3 Noise temperature & noise figure

Two currencies for the same sin

1.3 The 290 K fine print

The convention is useful, universal — and slightly false everywhere

F is defined against a 290 K source. Point a dish at 50 K of cold sky and an "NF = 1 dB" receiver (Te = 75 K) is not a 1 dB penalty — it is a 2.4× degradation of system noise. Satellite & radio-astronomy people budget in Te and kelvin for exactly this reason.

The attenuator theorem: a matched lossy passive at 290 K has NF = its loss in dB — it eats signal and replaces the lost noise with its own thermal emission. Prices every cable and filter tonight; sharp end of hour 3's deliberate bug.

Pre-empted question: "so is NF just loss?" — only for matched passives at 290 K. For anything with gain, F is a measured personality.

1.4 Friis 1944

The cascade formula — refer everything to the front door

F = F₁ + (F₂ − 1)/G₁ + (F₃ − 1)/G₁G₂ + …

Not a dB formula. Every F and G in it is linear. Hour 3 feeds it decibels on purpose — and it lies plausibly.

1.4 Friis 1944 · first principles

Why it's true: watts add, so temperatures add

Tsys = T₁ + T₂/G₁ + T₃/(G₁G₂) + …    then Ti = T₀(Fi − 1) → the F form
  • Noise powers are powers: they add in watts at any plane. Divide by the gain that brought them there.
  • The formula says one thing: gain ahead of you forgives your sins.

Read the original — Friis, "Noise Figures of Radio Receivers," Proc. IRE 1944 [R20]: four pages, assigned whole. He defines NF so competing receivers can be compared at all, then derives the cascade in half a page. His notation is ours; eighty years later you apply it unchanged tonight.

1.5 Antenna temperature & G/T

The source has a temperature too

  • Antenna at the ground: Tant ≈ 290 K. Clear zenith sky: tens of K. The sun: thousands.
  • System noise: Tsys = Tant + Te — referenced at the same plane (be pedantic; spec sheets are).

G/T (gain over system temperature, dB/K): the receiver half of any link budget in one number. Course radar, mast chain, Tant = 100 K: Te = 207 K → Tsys = 307 K → G/T = 8.1 dB/K.

Buying 3 dB of G/T with a bigger dish = steel and wind load. Buying it with Te = one better LNA. That asymmetry is why low-noise design is a profession — and why lecture 11 exists.

1.6 Recap

Hour 1 in three sentences

  • Available noise power is kTB and the R cancels — one floor for all receivers, −174 dBm/Hz, with 290 K fine print.
  • F and Te are two currencies for one fact; matched passives at 290 K have NF = loss.
  • Friis 1944: the first gain forgives everything behind it — 2.34 vs 5.38 dB from ordering the same five parts.

Hour 2

Nonlinearity, the Fiction
Called IP3, and the Squeeze

Taylor series → spurs next door · slope 3 and the extrapolated crossing · SFDR drawn · why the best chain is a judgment call

2.1 Nonlinearity is a Taylor series

One tone in — every RF (radio-frequency) phenomenon out

y = a₁x + a₂x² + a₃x³ + …   (a₁ = the gain you paid for; a₃ < 0 in compression)
  • x = A·cos ωt through x²: DC shift + 2nd harmonic at 2ω — far away; the filter eats it.
  • Through x³: 3rd harmonic at 3ω — far away — and a term at the fundamental: (3/4)a₃A³ → gain compression.
  • P1dB: the input where gain has sagged 1 dB — every datasheet's first nonlinearity number. For a pure cubic: P1dB ≈ IIP3 − 9.6 dB.

Memoryless + narrowband is the honest regime for this model; a₂'s products are out of band, which is why third order is about to become the villain.

2.1 Two tones — intermodulation

The cube puts spurs next door

x = A(cos ω₁t + cos ω₂t); the cube's cross terms 3·cos²ω₁t·cos ω₂t land at

2f₁ − f₂ and 2f₂ − f₁,  amplitude (3/4)a₃A³
  • IMD3 (third-order intermodulation distortion): tones 100 kHz apart → spurs 100 kHz outside them — in-band, unfilterable, born after every filter you own.
  • 2nd-order products (f₁+f₂, f₂−f₁): far away — filterable, forgivable.
2.2 IP3 — the extrapolated fiction

Two slopes must cross — nothing survives to the crossing

  • Fundamental: slope 1. IMD3: slope 3 — raise both tones 1 dB, spurs jump 3, gap closes 2.
  • The extrapolated crossing: IP3 — input-referred IIP3 or output OIP3 = IIP3 + gain.
  • Real amplifiers compress ~9.6 dB below it (our −5 dBm LNA: P1dB = −14.6 dBm in).

The fiction predicts real spurs:
spur below carrier = 2·(IIP3 − Pin).
Tones at −30 dBm, IIP3 −5 dBm → spurs 50 dB down. Hour 3 measures 49.9.

2.2 Cascade IIP3 — Friis's mirror twin

Gain behind you forgives noise; gain ahead of you aggravates distortion

1/IP3 = 1/IP3₁ + G₁/IP3₂ + G₁G₂/IP3₃ + …

all linear, input-referred, spur voltages adding in phase (the honest worst case); passives contribute 1/∞ = 0

Noise punishes what is early and lossy.
Distortion punishes what is late and driven.

Mast-chain blame table (hour 3 prints it): the LNA tops both columns — F term 1.41 of 1.71, and 3.16 of 5.47 mW⁻¹ in 1/IP3. The same 20 dB that hides the mixer's noise exposes its linearity.

2.3 SFDR — derived and drawn

Both ceilings on one axis

  1. MDS (minimum detectable signal) = −174 + 10log₁₀B + NF dBm (SNR = 0 convention)
  2. Spurs (input-referred): Pspur = 3Pin − 2·IIP3
  3. Set Pspur = MDS → Pin,max = (2·IIP3 + MDS)/3
SFDR = (2/3)·(IIP3 − MDS)

Mast chain, B = 1 MHz: MDS = −111.64 dBm, Pin,max = −42.13 dBm, SFDR = 69.5 dB. The 2/3 is the 3:1 slope geometry — nothing more.

2.3 SFDR fine print

The 2/3 has consequences

Widen B by 10 dB → MDS rises 10 dB, but SFDR falls only 6.67 dB. Measured: 89.51 / 69.51 / 56.17 dB at 1 kHz / 1 MHz / 100 MHz. One floor shift, three different exponents — homework Q5 collects all three.

For radar, the two tones are two targets: a truck near the radar and a drone at the edge of the beam. An SFDR shortfall paints phantom targets between them — false returns your own receiver manufactured, indistinguishable until you check the spur arithmetic. L14's CFAR (constant false-alarm rate) inherits every ghost made here.

2.4 The linearity–noise squeeze

The sentence that defines receiver design

Sensitivity wants maximum gain as early as possible; linearity wants minimum gain as late as possible. You cannot fully have both.

  • More LNA gain → NF ↓ and IIP3 ↓. A pad or filter up front → IIP3 ↑ dB-for-dB and NF ↑ dB-for-dB.
  • Tonight's shootout, quantified: best-MDS chain ≠ best-SFDR chain — 0.30 dB apart in one currency, 0.44 dB in the other. Choosing is a judgment about the threat environment.

The homework's planted "obvious" chain — filter first to protect the LNA — spends 3.04 dB of NF to buy out-of-band linearity headroom. Sometimes that is even right; Q4 makes you argue both sides.

2.4 Measuring NF and IP3 — in concept

A ratio thermometer and a spur ruler

Y-factor (NF): a calibrated noise diode — its violence quoted as ENR (excess noise ratio) — switched hot/cold at the input. One power ratio Y, then F = ENR/(Y − 1).

Hour 3, four lines: ENR 15 dB, our 1.5 dB LNA → Y = 13.69 dB → NF = 1.5000 recovered. Two power readings and a division — no absolute calibration.

Two-tone (IP3): two clean generators, one spectrum analyzer. Confirm the spurs ride slope 3, then extrapolate.

Trap: is the spur from the device — or from the analyzer's own mixer? Pad the analyzer 10 dB: spur drops 10 → real; drops 30 → the instrument confessing. Instruments obey the same Taylor series.

2.4 War story

The "improved" front end

  • Coastal radar, original build: LNA in a weatherproof box at the antenna — mast chain, NF 2.3 dB.
  • Modernization: LNA moved indoors (no ladder to service it), band filter moved ahead of it for lightning protection. Cable → filter → LNA: NF 5.4 dB.
  • 3 dB of system NF — half the sensitivity — gone, with no failed part anywhere. Ranges quietly 16% short (3 dB across R⁴); blamed on weather for a season.

Nobody changed a part number, so nobody re-ran Friis. The block diagram is a design document, and its order is load-bearing. Tonight your checker convicts this exact chain.

2.5 Recap

Hour 2 in three sentences

  • Cubes put spurs next door, at slope 3 — and IP3 is the fiction that prices them.
  • The cascade formulas are mirror twins: noise forgiven by gain behind, distortion aggravated by gain ahead.
  • SFDR = (2/3)(IIP3 − MDS) — 69.5 dB for the mast chain in 1 MHz — and the squeeze makes "best" a judgment call.
